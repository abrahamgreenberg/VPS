## On Writing

<p class="lead">My heart actually sank when I heard that I would have to take another writing class. I immediately thought of my experiences taking my English GCSE<sup><a href="#a-1">1</a></sup> in high school. The terrors of studying English literature: Ok, but did he actually mean that when he wrote it?</p>

I had always viewed myself as a creative enough person; I loved music and photography, and I had even edited some creative videos for friends and family. Yet I never thought of myself as a writer. It was just something I wasn’t suited for, I told myself.

I remember on the first day of class, I brought up the gripe of handwriting. All schoolwork and exams had to be handwritten, and I never really had beautiful, neat handwriting like some of my friends. In primary school, we had what was called a “pen-license”, which granted permission to students who could write legibly to write with a pen. I only received my pen license at the end of the year out of pity, because my teachers would rather we at least had some exposure to using a pen before we went off to high school. Upon reflection, the root of my gripe was not whether I could use a pen. It was a frustration with not being understood. When I edited a video that gave people goosebumps, made them cry, or made them laugh, I knew I had gotten my point across. I had never been able to add such a complexion to my writing.

“Why do I even need a writing class?” I thought. I’ve worked as a software engineer for the past few months. The only writing I had to do was a quick yes-or-no email, maybe a bit of technical documentation, though that’s pretty much all done with AI nowadays, and code. When would I ever need to write a personal narrative in a business email? Exploratory writing, pfft, just give them the answer, simple as that!

I had studied Maths, Further Maths, Physics, and Computer Science for my A-Levels<sup><a href="#a-2">2</a></sup>. As I got older, the range of subjects I studied narrowed, and naturally, I expected to specialize even more in College. While I may have been able to put off writing in the British education system, I certainly wasn’t going to be able to pull it off at Yeshiva University. The concept of cores and extra requirements that fall outside your degree, making maths students take writing classes, and English students take math classes, was admittedly quite overwhelming.

Suddenly, a problem that I had been shielded from in my recent few years of education had just become something I had to overcome. And to be honest, I’m glad it did. Even in my first semester, when I studied economics, history, and writing, I noticed improvements in my overall education and my ability to present myself. I have found a style I am more comfortable with, finding it easier to express my thoughts explicitly rather than trying to engineer a few words into a clumsily written paragraph.

During my exploratory essay, I enjoyed being able to say, “I don’t know, let’s find out the answer together”, rather than boiling down the problem into logic, statistics, and probability. They still found a place in my writing, but they were used to enhance my message and answer rather than as a binary yes-or-no. To me, good writing has come to mean the formulation and reasoning behind your ideas, with reliable sources and well-researched answers, rather than coming up with a fun story and using literary techniques.

I still recognize the tremendous value in such work, but I’ve tried it, and it wasn’t for me. I feel like I’ve discovered the mathematical solution to writing, a new style that I was never exposed to. That is why I can confidently say that I am grateful for my FYWR course, and actually hope to continue writing, with plenty of math and coding, of course.
